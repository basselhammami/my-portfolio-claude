Designing for certainty

How UX shaped the CLM Platform, RAKBANK's compliance control system.

Senior Product Designer Oct 2024 – Present R&D · Compliance Web · Internal Tools

Context

Less a productivity tool, more a control system

CLM is the layer where the bank's compliance decisions get made, tracked, and defended. It pulls customer data from Finacle, the bank's core system of record, and runs its workflows through IBPS, the process engine. CLM sits on top of both: the single place a review actually happens.

Before it existed, the teams who check customers against regulation worked across Finacle, IBPS, separate inboxes, and spreadsheets — systems with no common layer between them. Every handover blurred who was responsible. And when an audit came, reconstructing what had happened was the hardest part of the job.

The insight

What research actually told us

We walked in assuming staff wanted to move faster. Research told us the opposite. Interview after interview surfaced the same pattern — and it reset the entire design.

Reviewers aren't optimising for speed. They're optimising for defensibility — the fear isn't missing a deadline, it's approving something they shouldn't have.

What every reviewer interview surfaced

The feature

One feature carries that insight: the audit trail

If defensibility is what reviewers are really optimising for, the most important surface in the platform isn't the queue or the dashboard. It's the record a decision leaves behind. So that's the feature this case study walks through — in the two halves it's designed around: how the record gets written, and how it gets read.

Part 01 · Writing the record

How much should staff be asked to justify?

A trail is only as good as what goes into it. The core design decision was how much reasoning to demand from people at the moment of action:

  • Silent logging. Every action recorded automatically, no user input needed.
  • Full justification. Every action requires a typed reason before proceeding.
  • Structured remarks. Required reasoning only at routing changes, risk overrides, and consequence triggers.

We ruled out silent logging because it produces a record but not a narrative: you can see what happened, but not why. We ruled out full justification because it creates friction at high volume and produces low-quality free text nobody reads.

We landed on structured remarks at the moments that matter. When a reviewer routes a case, overrides a risk score, or triggers a consequence, the decision panel asks for a reason — structured, short, and attached to the action itself.

Part 02 · Reading the record

The full story of a case, in one panel

The audit trail opens over the case itself — no separate report, no export, no reconstruction. Every system event and every human action sits in one timeline: who did it, when, and the remark that explains why. A risk override reads differently from a routine routing step, because the trail carries the reasoning, not just the event.

It serves two readers at once. For an auditor, it's the evidence: a review that can be replayed end to end. For a reviewer picking up a case, it's reassurance: someone else already checked this, and here's what they saw.

What it changed

From reconstructing decisions to replaying them

The audit trail is the difference between a system that logs decisions and one that defends them. What used to be the hardest part of an audit — piecing together what happened across four systems — is now a panel you open. And because the record is built at the moment of action, not assembled after the fact, it's the one account of a case everyone trusts: reviewers, team leads, and the auditors they answer to.
